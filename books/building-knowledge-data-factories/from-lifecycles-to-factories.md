A single data lifecycle is important to understand as well as the analogy of moving data from raw and unfiltered through steps to make it consumable. In enterprises the production of data often is taking to different scale and that is where i like to enrich our mental model with something from the physical world. 
### From raw materials to finished goods
In 1992, when data warehousing was still young, Bill Inmon published *The Corporate Information Factory*. To me this is a very clever title as i see many parallels between how manufacturers produce physical goods from raw materials like wood, iron, copper, fabric. Some of these materials may come directly from nature but often also from upstream suppliers. These flow through the supply chain and value chain of the company where they are shaped, combined, refined and in that process turned into products that serve a purpose in someone’s life. That is how furniture, clothes, machinery and the computer i am typing on right now is introduced to the market. Firms that add the most value to the customers throughout often ends up with the most popular products so it's very important to understand what activities are value-adding and which are not. 

The information factory have a similar structure and also often operate at a large scale. Raw data is collected from logs, transactions, sensor readings, clicks. Leaving them untouched in it's raw form typically means no one will use it, because it's not consumable. That's why the data warehouse came to be as it became the factory through which data could be integrated, transformed and modeled into finished good, what we might call **data products**. Like manufacturing firms store goods at different stages of refinement: raw materials, work-in-progress, finished inventory. Modern data architectures mirror this logic. Whether we call it staging, bronze–silver–gold, or something else entirely, the structure reflects a familiar truth: value increases as raw inputs are shaped into usable artifacts.

Going from a single lifecycle to the factory helps understand the scale at which enterprise data systems operate. There are also many other similarities that i find very useful when it comes to constructing the mental model at the enterprise scale. 
### Scaling the data factory
Enterprise production of data assets often requires large scale production and like physical systems that require investments. As volumes grow and use cases multiply, organizations invest in new platforms, distributed storage, orchestration tools, and automation. Like a manufacturer that needs to scale up, the digital architecture evolves. Workflows become more formalized. Informal scripts give way to managed *pipelines*. Many of the ideas of physical manufacturing is spilling over to data production such as _kaizen_—continuous improvement—and methodologies like _lean_ and Six Sigma. These approaches are there to make small improvements over time, reduce waste, limit variability, and increase reliability something that compound into significant gains. Data engineers have the responsibility to make data quality tests, pipeline monitoring and small continuous improvements to reap some of the same benefits. They are the equivalents of inspection stations and standardized work procedures and term _DataOps_ captures this convergence: applying operational rigor to the production of data products.

The payoff is familiar. Shorter cycle times from source to insight. Greater trust in outputs as data quality is higher. The ability to scale without chaos.
### Orchestrating production systems
Data flows through the systems in the business every single day and so our pipelines have to run regularly to keep the products up to date. In physical production systems we often see production lines moving the raw materials through different stages which is *orchestrated* by the natural dependencies that exists and designed to move accomplish work efficiently. Data centric manufacturing systems are no different. The system have dependencies where the *bronze*, *silver*, *gold* phasing is a popular design choice and the pipeline runs in that order. With scale it becomes a priority to make the system as efficient as possible. The design of the information factory therefore also means considering:
- Which processes need to happen first? What is the order in which things should happen? What are the dependencies?
- How can we built quality assurance into the physical design? For example can we run certain activities in parallel to increase throughput? What other ways can we optimize the flow of goods and services?
- How can we automate processes as much as possible, such that things are scheduled and automatically started by certain signals?

Instead of people and heavy machinery moving things around is is instead clusters of CPUs that move and transform the data in the pipelines. Most data warehouse solutions have some built-in orchestration tools that enable creation of pipelines and mechanisms for creating automatic triggers. They also have the option to pass parameters to make pipelines dynamic and reusable. For example the same ingestion pipeline can work for multiple databases by parameterizing the connection and authentication strings. The same transformation pipeline can handle multiple tables by having a table name parameter. 

While many enterprises are building their knowledge and data-centric factory around tools and technologies that allow for scaling and effective production of data products, there are some cases where simple setups are most suitable. A simple spreadsheet, no fancy orchestration tools can be a very effective data product if it's put to productive use, which is really what we should measure success on. The choice of architecture and tools depends on the context such as resources available, the prior experience of business people, analytical maturity and so on. 
### Value through use
There are also stories from the physical world that helps us reaffirm that we should not skip the requirements definition and ideation phases and why data-driven operating models all include this element. 

A product creates value only when it is used. A kettle is valuable when it boils water. A car when it moves us from A to B. A piece of furniture when someone sits in it. Utility is realized in action.
The same holds for data. A dashboard unread is no different from unsold inventory. A model no one trusts might as well not exist. Data products generate value only when they enter the decision stream and alter behavior. Consider the well-known failure of the Ford Edsel. Introduced in 1957 with enormous fanfare, the Edsel was meant to compete with mid-priced offerings from General Motors and Chrysler. Ford invested heavily—financially and reputationally. The launch was dramatic. Expectations were high. Yet the market response was tepid, even hostile. Sales fell far below projections, and within a few years the model was discontinued at a substantial loss.

The Edsel was not a failure of manufacturing capacity. It was a failure of product–market fit. However much research had been claimed, the product did not resonate with users. It did not create sufficient value in their lives. Data has its own Edsels. These are the initiatives that ingest, transform, and model vast quantities of information—only to discover that it cannot be put to productive use. The design may be elegant. The pipelines may be robust. The architecture may follow best practice. But if the solution does not improve decisions, it is ornamental. The financial losses in data projects rarely reach the scale of Ford’s misstep, but the damage is real. Wasted time. Burned trust. Analytical fatigue. A growing skepticism toward the next “strategic” initiative.

This is why requirements, prototyping, and user feedback are not bureaucratic hurdles; they are safeguards against irrelevance. Designing data products demands the same discipline as designing physical ones. Who is the user? What decision will change? What behavior will shift? At what moment in their workflow? Without clear answers, we are merely producing inventory. That's why it's important to understand the entire system so that the data becomes a natural part of work and thereby a feedback loop is integrated.
