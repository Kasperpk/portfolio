The birth of a data product follows a process that has been given many names. It could be *the journey to wisdom* or the more technical terms *extract-transform-load* or ETL. 

Let's look closer into what the data lifecycle consist of. 
## Understanding the data lifecycle
From the earliest decision support systems it became obvious that data would need to follow a journey analogous of how water is extracted and distributed. In the book *Architecting Data and Machine Learning*, the authors name this process very fittingly *the journey to wisdom* and figure 2.6 is an adapted or modified sketch from their book where a similar one can be found. In a typical water system the process usually begins by locating some underground source of water which then can be pumped up. The water is pumped into a well from where it centrally can be cleaned and filtered for distribution to end consumers. 

As we start manufacturing the data product we have designed, we typically start by collecting the data. This is exactly what Matthew Benham's team at SmartOdds did when they put a team of watchers on stadiums to record live data. It could be that the data does not exists yet, however a lot of data is generated inside existing *source systems* which is the umbrella term for all the locations in which data can be generated and stored. Benham reminds us that we shouldn't confine ourselves to already exists in fact, the most valuable solutions might come from capturing not previously collected data. With that said being acquainted with what exists can be a good thing. 

A common journey taps into source systems of large dedicated cloud or local servers that are geared for the particular purpose of supporting database applications. These are the applications which are generating data in large volumes and the database underneath is like the computerized version of physical record systems. The *raw* data which is generated is the equivalent to unfiltered water found at various underground sources. This raw version typically needs a bit of work before it can become a fully functioning data product. Therefore the process often moves onto to a transformation stage where it is shaped into final building blocks. The transformation phase is like the filtering and cleansing which happens in a water plant which prepares the water to be distributed out to end users. 

![[Journey to wisdom.png|center]]
Figure 3.1 Journey to wisdom adapted from Architecting Data and Machine Learning platforms

The final stage is where the mechanisms of generating data value is *activated*. That is when information enters the system on the other side and becomes part of a solution to a business problem. In enterprises the volumes of data are typically larger and the number of source systems greater. Data typically needs to be integrated and processes using dedicated tools which means *ingesting* data to a central repositories using different ingestion methods to copy data over public or private networks. 

However the journey itself can take many forms. One may have their own spreadsheet with some manual data insertions thus performing *ingestion* by hand. The suite of source systems can also include anything from hard drives on personal laptops, peripheral devices, our own minds and anything we can think of using to generate and collect data. That person may insert data exactly as desired without having to transform it later or integrate it with other sources. The activation might also simply be happening directly through looking up different information in the spreadsheet. In this way the central repository or *data warehouse* is the persons own hard drive on the local computer. Even the smallest and simplest workflows using data can be defined in terms of the stages of the journey to wisdom. 

It's important not to neglect this type of workflow because simplicity is ultimately sophistication. We should always try to make things as simple as possible and not spin up large clusters in the cloud or make huge pieces of software unless it fits the context. Instead the flexibility and fast hands-on with data can be the best solution and often a good way to prototype if needs and requirements are difficult to define. The problem is when these types of information systems begin to dominate the systems landscape and also supports important decisions. In the rest of this chapter i will be sitting aside those simplistic and often personal data journeys and instead focus on the reality that many organizations lives in.

Finally i want to bring up an often overseen part of the data cycle, which is *disposing*. We should try to consider how long data should be retained. There might be rules and regulations for how long you can keep sensitive data which creates a hard deadline. However even if it's not a regulatory requirement it's still an important question. Even as storage has become exceptionally cheap we don't want to stock up on data that no longer generates value. For whatever reason data can get outdated. It may be that as time passes, the value of the data decreases, eventually close to zero. In some contexts this degrading happens very quickly while in others the value of the original data points remain intact. In some cases we might keep data for longer in the raw and intermediate versions while applying filters to the end products which are queried so to make performance better for the end user.

## Databases and the data underground 
Every data product that is delivered has an origin story and commonly the word *source system* is used as an umbrella term to describe where the data is originally generated. In a typical enterprise the landscape of source systems may be a combination of 

* Operational source systems (OLTP, Applications) which usually supports some business process and the data is generated from those activities. 
* Machine generated sources (IoT, Logs, Telemetry) which typically is generated in high volume and with event timestamps. 
* Human generated sources in the form of spreadsheets, forms and APIs. These are very flexible and can close gaps that are in the more large scale source systems. They can be extremely valuable but also error-prone and create something "spider-web" like if not carefully managed. 

As data professionals and engineers we should view the umbrella of source systems as our gold mines. These are the applications that represent some physical reality and interact with the events, signals and records that can help describe the reality of the business. One of the lessons we also can learn from Matthew Benham's innovative use of data is that it is possible to mold and shape the data foundation we have. ### Data intensive source systems
As businesses have become ever more digital it means that they use more and more applications to run and manage their business. These applications often provide nice graphical user interface to control, manage and run some operations. For example economic systems needs to be able to create new customers, retailers update their inventory with each transaction, payroll systems needs to be able to fill in new employee information and the manufacturing systems needs to be able to create new production plans. Underneath these applications we find special pieces of software that are designed to handle this data that is generated as the business go by it's day to day operations, called databases. it has not always been that way. For example in the very infant time of digital computing, data was stored on something called punch cards or magnetic disks which supported simple file systems. These simple file systems required very customized software and in general the number of business applications that this could support was very limited. Several inventions came along so that in 1966 IBM could introduce the very first database management systems or DBMS for short. More formally we might define a database as a "collection of related data" (p. 4) and the DBMS is designed to store large amounts of data in databases and manage that data effectively, laying the foundation for data intensive applications. In IBM's database they structured data hierarchically, represented by a tree and that became the first abstract *data model* allowing developers to focus on the logical representation of data separately from implementation. The DBMS software is not only an abstracting over storing data on disks. It is also a system that enables interaction between the database and end users by introducing expression languages to locate, delete and in general manage the data in the database. Secondly it helps manage data by keeping track of what is stored in the database through a metadata store that is separate from the actual data stored. This metadata describes the data stored in the primary database both in terms of the relations and all the attributes which belong to that relation along with their data type. The DBMS software package introduces functionalities which distinguishes it from file-system processing by including features such as isolation and atomicity. These features makes sure that each multiple *transactions* (insert, update, retrieve) can be performed on the database at the same time. This allows multiple clients to interact with the database at the same time, something which is necessary for many business applications where many people use the applications. The database systems also comes with features such as indexing, caching and query optimizations that improves the user experience by allowing faster search of data on disks and faster query results. Additionally backups and data validations provides possibility for recovery of data. DBMS are complex pieces of software with many different components which allows all these features to coexists. ### The relational database model
The relational model is a very nice abstraction that presents to the user data in logical groupings called **relations** or tables. This is quite far from the physical implementation in hardware but is extremely logical for people and it is closely related to mathematical logic Codd grounded his ideas in set theory and first-order logic. A relation can be thought of as a *set* a mathematically concept which contains unique elements that share some characteristics. When the set is a relation in a database then each unique element represents a tuple or row that describe the attributes of one specific element. A field is then a specific cell that intersects a row and a column, something we often are familiar with from spreadsheets. For examples we may have the set $Customers$ with the tuple $(id, name, city)$. A single element could then be describes as $(1, Peter, Birmingham)$. The user can query the data in the relational database which we may express mathematically with relational algebra. For example we may write an operation as $\sigma_c(C)$. Which means select all customers where some condition $c$ is true. For example if we wanted to select all the customers from Italy we could write $\sigma_{country} = \space 'Italy' \space (Customers)$. As mentioned SQL is the primary language and to query data we define a `SELECT` statement that chooses a subset of the entire set or combination of sets which satisfies some condition. The above query could be written in SQL as `SELECT * FROM CUSTOMERS WHERE Country = 'Italy'`. There are many other operations we can do in the relational model which makes up the data query language (DQL) which is the part of SQL that contains the functionality to query data. Every such operation take one or more relation as input and produce a relation as output. The basic operations include selection as we have seen but also operations to select specific columns, combining two relations a single union or cartesian product and finding the difference between the relations corresponding to finding the set difference in mathematical logic. A projection written in relational algebra that selects the columns name and address attributes from the customer relation may look like this $\pi_{(N,A)}(C)$. In SQL `SELECT Name, Customer from Customers`. The union operation may combine two tables with the same or similar attributes. This is more often used in analytical databases where multiple sources often are combined. Nonetheless it can look like this $(CustomersNew) U (CustomersOld)$ where supposedly combine some new customer table with an old one. The operation in SQL uses the `UNION` command. Notice the * is used to signal that all columns are projected. 

```
SELECT * 
	FROM CustomersNew
UNION
SELECT *
	FROM CustomersOld
```

The set difference is used to find the elements of some relation that are not in another relation using the (-) sign. For example $(CustomersNew) - (CustomersOld)$ finds the new customers that are not also in the old customers table. In SQL the `EXCEPT` command is used to perform this operation. 

```
SELECT * 
	FROM CustomersNew
EXCEPT
SELECT *
	FROM CustomersOld
```

The cartesian product is an operation that combines every row in one relation with every row in another. There may be many uses for this when using and querying the data available in the database. The $X$ sign is used so two relations $C$ and $B$ are combined into a cartesian product mathematically expressed as $C \space X \space B$. Let $B$ be some relation `BankAccounts` then the SQL implementation is as follows using the `CROSS JOIN` command 

```
SELECT *
	FROM CustomersNew
CROSS JOIN
	BankAccounts
```

In addition to the basic operations there are also some derived operations which combine the basic ones to perform more complex ones. This includes intersection, division and different join types which are very useful particularly when it comes to making insights and answering questions of the data in the database. There are other parts of the SQL language such as the data definition language (DDL) used to create, alter and delete data elements, data manipulation language (DML) used to insert, update and delete data, data control language (DCL) used to grant and revoke access rights and permissions to objects in the database. Finally there is transaction control language (TCL) used to group a set of tasks into a single execution unit. Different types of users will use different languages. For example database administrators may use DDL, DCL and TCL while users of applications will use query options and manipulation often through the interface of their application. 

**Keeping integrity in the database**
One of the important concepts of the RDBMS is to enforce integrity. **Domain integrity** can be explained as the rules that ensures data is consistent and accurate. For example if the data type of some attribute is an integer, then new inserts into that table has to be an integer for that attribute. The second type of integrity is **entity integrity**. It's necessary that each row or tuple in a table is unique. This is ensured by creating a key for each row which much be unique and cannot contain NULL. A **primary key** is the column chosen for a specific table which hold the uniqueness and not null constraint. Sometimes it can be combination of columns that make up what's called a **composite key** and then there must be uniqueness for that combination of attributes. Thirdly we have to make sure that rules of relationships between tables are followed. In the RDBMS this is called **referential integrity**. Relationships between tables can be seen as parent-child relationships and when you want to link a parent to a child, you refer to the child by putting the primary key of it's parent in the child table as a **foreign key**. This integrity type is called and for example makes sure you can't delete a row from a parent table which references some child table.

**Normalizing data in the database**
When we work with databases we may take it for granted that the data is structured fairly good. In the relational database the concept of normalization ensures that data is possible to work with. In this example i will try to show how important the way we structure data is and how normalization is giving us clear rules to follow. Consider for example the example data in the table 3.5 below.

Here data has been clumped together into a single table. There are fields such as accounts where multiple values are inserted. How would you query to see the balance of the customers savings accounts? That would be quite a headache. Codd wanted to eliminate that in what he called the **first normal form** (1NF). His normalization procedure in general follows a hierarchy of normal forms starting from 1NF before moving to subsequent forms. To adhere to first normal form we are going to eliminate those repeated groups. This is done by splitting the data into two relations and creating a new row for each account and loan that a customer has. 

![[Loan and Accounts table.jpeg]]
Figure 3.6 Example of data in first normal form

Now the data is still not quite normalized to the extend that we desire in relational databases. The  **second-normal form** (2NF) removes partial dependencies which means we split entities out so that the attributes depend only on the primary key of that table. That means we now have five separate relations closely mirroring our logical data model. 

![[2NF tables.jpeg]]
Figure 3.7 Example of data in second and third normal form

Now at this point the data is normalized also adhering to **third normal form** (3NF) which would remove any transitive dependencies for which there is no in this example. That would have been the case if there was a  non-key attribute that depended on another non-key attribute. For example if the birthdate of the customer was placed in the account table then that would have been a violation to the third-normal form. There exists more constraint forms of normalization but for the most part 3NF is the one that relational databases adhere to. The result of normalizing is data structured in a way that is much easier to maintain and work with. For example if some department change name or type, we only have to change it one places and all subsequent queries will show the change. Similarly if a new customer was created but no account was assigned to it yet, the one-big-table structure would have trouble representing that meaningfully. In general we can say that normalization doesn’t remove data — it removes unnecessary repetition by making dependencies explicit.

## Data warehouses and the data well
As workers began to demand and use data in businesses they would extract that data. Bill Inmon describes in his book *Building the Data Warehouse* how this would be done in an ad-hoc manner using various "extract jobs". On top of that data they may have been building reports and manipulating the data in some way. That was kind of how working with data in businesses started. However this created an accidental architecture as Inmon refers to as the "spider web" or "out-of-control extract processing". This was not a winning strategy for succeeding as it quickly became messy. Instead this motivated him and other pioneers to “pump” or ingest this data into a central repository using dedicated tools. Data was landed in a shared repository or "data well" to stay within the analogy and became known as the *data warehouse* architecture. Not only did this serve as a unified and more credible way of serving data, it also made it possible to combine the data with other sources. Most businesses that work with data have invested in such technologies however there are the companies so small that they don't have systems that generate data or that generate enough interesting data for them to take that route. They may keep a few local spreadsheets with the most important economic data which could be manually inserted. 

There is also a third reason for the invention of the data warehouse. A DBMS system designed for operational applications also called online transactional processing (OLTP) has demands on them which are alleviated by for example introducing indexing algorithms. A typical operation is to insert, update or access a few rows which can be done efficiently with clever indexing and hash keys. It's also an important requirement in operational databases that the data is quickly altered or inserted so it reflects the current reality of the business immediately afterwards. Analytical queries on data such as "*what was the total amount of sales last month?*" requires retrieving larger subset of data and calculating aggregates, something that the clever indexing algorithms above do not solve particularly well. With analytics the need for having real-time updates of data is more a nice to have than a nice to have in most cases. For example if you look at sales over a month then the last 10 minutes of data does not weight as much as the past 30 days. Because the demands and data retrieval patterns are so different it was again the best option to separate transactional storage from analytical. When looking inside the analytical database we can spot this difference for example often in the way data is laid out. In a transactional database data will be stored row by row where all the attributes of a single row are next to each other. This is very nice when we want to retrieve or update a few rows because they are located close to each other on the disk. However in a analytical query we often decide on a few attributes but more rows such as transactions the last 30 days. Therefore analytical databases are often stored column-oriented for example storing the actual sales amount column in the same file. To make this work the order has to be preserved in the row so the 25th row in the one column file can be combined with 25th row in another file to assemble an entire row. (p. 97, Kleppmann) Popular file formats in analytical databases such as parquet and delta tables are using columnar storage format together with compression algorithms to optimize storage and query performance. 
### Subject oriented, integrated, non-volatile, time-invariant enterprise data
In came the data warehouse which Bill Inmon defined as the subject-oriented, integrated, non-volatile and time-invariant store of data. The data warehouse is an architecture and not a technology and in the early stages there where some debate about how it should look like. In 1996 Ralph Kimball published the book "*The Data Warehouse Toolkit: Practical Techniques for Dimensional Data Warehouses*". In the same period Bill Inmon published books on data warehousing such as the *Corporate Information Factory* in 1998 and *Building the Data Warehouse* in 1992. Inmon disagreed with Kimball on how a data warehouse should be designed. Kimball proposed the idea of making the data warehouse a collection of ready made data marts to begin with. Inmon argued that data instead should be landed inside the data warehouse first before transforming it further. His argument was that the requirements across departments and business units were so diverse that it should be possible to mold the same data in different ways to build data marts from the same set of raw materials. ### From data warehouse to data intelligence platforms and back
There has been several evolutions to the way of designing and implementing data warehouse solutions. Vendors like Microsoft, IBM and Oracle started offering software products like Microsoft SQL Server, Oracle Exadata or IBM Db2 Warehouse which became cornerstone in this area. Microsoft's SQL Server Integration Services (SSIS), Oracle’s Data Integrator (ODI) and IBM’s DataStage complemented the warehouse by allowing extraction of data from various sources and also transformations which together became known as ETL or extract-transform-load. With new capabilities within storage and compute new versions and technologies have been introduced to the market. The data lake term was introduced in the 2000s with Hadoop and MapReduce as the major technological drivers. Traditional data warehouse storage was robust at handling structured data and ingesting from relational databases. However as we have seen not all types of data fit that profile very well. The digital reality of businesses can exists in more unstructured formats that fit other types of databases such as document and graph databases. It may be files of text, sound, video and images or internet-of-things data something which the data lake could handle. Although the data lake with Hadoop and MapReduce won popularity it wasn't until the Spark project and the delta table storage format that we would see the next major overhaul of traditional data warehouses. One of the major innovations was the delta table, an open-source columnar storage format that provides ACID transactional capabilities by keeping a transaction log. Secondly the Spark project introduced improvements to the existing distributed computing engines enabling data tasks to be distributed among several compute nodes. The founders of Spark introduced the term Data Lakehouse and Inmon in 2021 co-authored *Building the Data Lakehouse* which rests on the shoulders of the pioneering work he, Kimball and others did on data warehousing. This Lakehouse architecture evolution allows companies to take advantage of all the many advancements in compute and storage emerging from the ideas of the data lake while keeping data warehouse concepts intact. Secondly Lakehouses integrate support for machine learning and infused artificial intelligence. Thirdly, the data lakehouse platform allows different languages to query and manipulate data. This is also convenient as for some types of work Python may offer needed capabilities, while those more comfortable in SQL can use that to query and manipulate data.

### Data processing architectures
Following the advice of Bill Inmon many organizations follow the idea of having a landing zone and transform that same raw data for different use-cases. Early on when storage was very limited and expensive this may have met more resistance but gradually people started to have a two-tier architecture from where they could built data marts or (hyper)cubes to serve different analytical needs. The more recent version of this idea is the **medallion architecture**. The medallion architecture keep at least three versions of data. Some use the words bronze, silver and gold, others use raw curated and transformed and a third group something totally different. The names and numbers of layers are not the important thing, instead the concepts of creating a reliable data processing framework which can serve the needs of the organizations as a whole and individual needs of single departments, business units and teams. The medallion architecture represented In Matt Palmer's book *Understanding ETL* visualizes the typical process where multiple sources are ingested and stored in different curated versions through bronze, silver and gold layers.

![](Data%20flow%20in%20ETL.png)
Figure 3.1 Data flow in ETL. Adapted from Matt Palmer, Understanding ETL (O’Reilly Media, Inc., 2024), 45.

As it may be visible it has many similarities with the data warehouse architecture and in many ways it's just a natural evolution reflecting changing demands, new technological advancements and learning from experience over the years. Piethein Strengholt the author of the book *Medallion architectures* discusses this architecture and provides some general guidelines. For example data in the bronze zone should be as Inmon would suggest touched as little as possible. As data moves through the data and knowledge centric factory it becomes refined often with some rough transformations in the silver layer where it's common to select, renaming and cast data types of columns. These transformations are typically so general that many data assets can be processed by the same generic jobs. In the gold layer as we shall see the data comes into the final pieces suitable for redeeming value from it. This is where each payload often receive dedicated processing as business logic is defined and data is harmonized across source systems. This is where in-depth business and data understanding becomes very valuable and where conversations with business people and analysts around requirements actually materialize. 

It's not to say that transforming data has to be done in this way. For smaller amounts of data it might not be necessary with three layers however in practice this has been working quite well for some time and provides a logical model for turning raw data into useful products. It's important to remember that these are some general guidelines and should not be followed on autopilot but to meet the needs of the organization who should be free to choice options for different design choices based on specific needs and preferences. 
## Refining data for monitoring stocks and understanding the business through data
It's very important to spend adequate time building the robust and reliable data foundation with bronze and silver layers. However for now i am going to focusing on the final stages of the medallion architecture. This is where the bulk of transformations in terms of business logic is applied. It's also typically at this stage where disparate data sources are combined under single enterprise source-of-truth data assets. 

Deliberate data modeling in the data warehouse have similar benefits to those who use ER-models when developing application software. There are many similarities because the entities and relationships from source systems still exists in the data warehouse, however there are a few differences. There are challenges which are unique to OLAP databases and data warehouses which both Kimball and Inmon presented solutions for. The solution became the *dimensional model*. The dimensional model helps anyone who designs data solutions keeping all the puzzle pieces in place and to get shared understanding with co-workers. When physically implemented the dimensional model can support a strong data product. 

Consider a hospitality business where the managers and employees may want to monitor room reservations and track their occupancy rate. They may wake up with questions like 

* “How many room reservations of each room type did we have in each week of the last month?”
* “What was the sale in the previous three months compared to the past three months?”
* “Which customer reviews did our luxury rooms get in the latest survey?” 

Those are analytical queries and not found in some hotel reservation system that is used to run the daily operations. Looking into the first question above we can identify two important entities including room reservations and a linked room. In addition we have from a data modeling perspective also a date or time entity linked to that reservation which is also expressed in the question.

### Dimensional modeling vocabulary
Being well versed in data modeling and participating in discussions about dimensional modeling is in my opinion a core skill for data professionals. Having been introduced to ER-models and relational database makes life easier for us. The concept of primary keys and foreign keys functions exactly the same as in relational databases. Cardinality and relationships likewise. For example in the hypothetical example of an hospitality business above each room needs to be assigned a unique identifier so when a reservations that reservation can link to that room. The reservation also needs a date so we can link it to some date dimension. 

However dimensional modelling have it's own names and also unique concepts. One of these is that the dimensional model distinguish between tables that hold transactional data and tables which record more stabile entity data. The transactional table is the one that populates the cells in the sketching figures above and the data is often numerical in nature, just like number of reservations. This type of table is called a **fact table** and often is the center of the model. Tables that surrounds the central fact table are called **dimensions** and describe the facts from different perspectives such as date, room type and so on. A simplistic sketch is shown in figure 2.11 and as you may agree it could look a bit like a star, why it's also referred to as a **star schema**.

![[Dimensional model|center]]
Figure 2.11 Example dimensional model Star Schema

Like in ER-models foreign keys exist to bind and connect data in the dimensional model. However the dimensional model often introduces a third key type called the *surrogate key*. These synthetic keys generated outside the source system. There are several reasons for this type of key. One reason is that primary keys in source systems may hold sensitive information like a social security number. It's not ideal in all cases that this is visible for users, sometimes it's forbidden by regulations. Secondly the primary key can be *composite* meaning it takes multiple columns to identify a single record and the key can become a large string and this is not optimal for making joins. The surrogate key solves this by hiding sensitive information and by being a integer which is the engine can work with much faster when making lookups and joins. 

Modeling data in the data warehouse comes with it own set of challenges and over time several useful techniques have been developed. The Kimball Group's website holds a glossary on many of those techniques at [Dimensional Modeling Techniques - Kimball Group](https://www.kimballgroup.com/data-warehouse-business-intelligence-resources/kimball-techniques/dimensional-modeling-techniques/). The dimensional model enters the stage quite early and Kimball introduced it early in the requirements gathering phase with *collaborative dimensional modeling workshops*. A data model always accompanies any data solution however it seems to be only few engineers who does data modeling deliberately. It's up for debate how one considers there to be enough benefit in data modeling deliberately however i am of the opinion that it's time worth spending. I believe that because data models make it's possible to keep all the puzzle pieces together which can be particularly difficult in enterprise data warehouses. 

The data model in the data warehouse can as data models for application software exist at the conceptual, logical and physical level. The conceptual model is no different than the conceptual ER-model. It describes how the business works through nouns and verbs. Such a model might exists already covering at least some of the enterprise which means it might be possible to get our hands on this  first phase in those workshops and requirements stages where we learn more about how the business works and it's puzzle pieces of data. It's really about spending some time understanding the problem we are trying to solve. 

Data modeling then follows into the next phases of development where we replace the conceptual with the logical model. The logical model is a closer representation to a dimensional model. It puts entities and relationships into dimensional modeling context with a central fact table holding the measurements and the surrounding dimensions. The logical model is created as a single star schema which covers a single solution. For example within a machinery domain there might be reporting and analysis needs of economic data, IoT sensor data and service data. Each of these servers their own analysis needs but share the same domain and a lot of dimensions. It makes sense to pack these into a single large data product but to keep the logical models as star schemas for each subdomain. 

These logical models are used to make the design of the final physical model which is the one implemented in the actual data platform. The physical model describes details of different tables and replaces the entities with actual table names like *dim_customer* or *fact_asset_entries*. In addition it describes the field names, data types and what the primary and foreign keys are on the tables. It might also include documentation of which are the owner of the product and some typical use cases. Once the model is physically implemented it functions as a living artifact which can be extended with new dimensions, new measurements as the company grows and finds new requirements. 

Not all datasets follow textbook like structures with very straightforward ways of connecting data to a dimensional model. Additionally people have found all sorts of requirements which has kept challenging the existing paradigm of data products. Difficult design problems such as many-to-many, complex hierarchies or different granularities have also meant that dimensional modeling has evolved over time. Many answers have been found to these challenging problems and hopefully will keep adapting as new tools, technologies and problems arise. 

The way people have been interacting with the dimensional model has also changed dramatically. In the early days a lot of analysis was purely tabular often limited to a few SQL experts to wrangle out results from databases. In 2010 Microsoft introduced DAX along with **PowerPivot for Excel 2010**. PowerPivot was an Excel extension which could connect to dimensional models or cubes.   This was quite revolutionary as DAX made it possible to enrich models in many creative ways. There are an almost unlimited number of ways to utilize formulas available in these OLAP compatible languages, creating a whole professional discipline in mastering them. 

Later in 2015 PowerBI was introduced, another Microsoft product which that was a real gamechanger in the field. Before that a lot of analytics was only tabular with results of SQL queries or manipulation of data in spreadsheets. When PowerBI came along with other visualization software, it was now possible to engage with dimensional models using graphs, diagrams and plots.  Important we do not use the dimensional model just because it seems to have worked on other problems, but instead for the reason that it is the method that most effectively meets the information needs of the user. More recently the introduction of AI means that dimensional models can be queried by agents allowing people to have conversational interfaces with the data. 

Dimensional models can also fuel model based analytics. In the very early days of decision support the word *data mining* emerged which described this model based approach to using data. With data mining and model based analytics, we exploring data in a more scientific way. This exercise have grown in to job roles of data science and *machine learning* which search for gold in data using statistical and mathematical methods.
