If the value of data was expressed by a function $f(x) = Ax$ where $x$ is the input measured in the information quality latent in it then $f(x)$ would not be static. The $A$ here is factor that is multiplied on which is going to be the sum of two things, technological advancement and accumulation of data. 

In the long history of the professional use of data several technological advances has amplified the value the data stored by organizations. The birth of data data discipline in businesses can be related back to the 1970s where the authors Scott Morton and Peter G.W. Keen published the book *Decision Support Systems: An organizational perspective*. This period was characterized by several major advances in the electronics industry perhaps most notably by invention of MOSFET transistors. Intel released it's first generation CPU or microprocessor, the intel 4004 in 1971 marking the start of a new era in computing technology. 

Sophistication from the hardware as well as the software side meant that many businesses could now introduce software applications to support their operations such as accounting, manufacturing and product design systems. Importantly these are all working with data. Workers were very keen on getting their hands on this data which now was stored in databases of the different applications supporting the business. It was scrappy work to get the data from these systems which was done with ad-hoc extract jobs resulting in much manual work. It eventually became known as the spider-web architecture as each person was finding their own way in to the systems. 

But technology did not stand still and one of the most central concepts, the data warehouse emerged as an architecture to replace the disparate spider-web architecture. Businesses now began investing in data warehouses installing the software on on-premise servers which could hold the data and also process, likely with *structured query language* (SQL). Data came out as query results in tables with rows and columns which perhaps is not the way we prefer to be presented information. 

In 2003 Tableau emerged and this was a dedicated business intelligence tools which could translate those query results into visuals which marked a shift in the way businesses would use data. The demand for storing data was growing and some organization begun building data centers which could store very large data volumes which became known as the *cloud*. It led to the term *big data* as most businesses now were no longer constrained by storage limits to the same extent. 

The historical perspective of the evolvement of technology is important because it has been increasing the $A$ parameter in the formula that opened this section. This has happened continuously as simple personal computing storage became databases and later data warehouses hosted in distributed cloud data centers. It has happened when simple operational analytics supported by decision support systems became advanced business intelligence software. Likewise it has happened when former analog statistical exercises could now be digitized allowing for sophisticated algorithms to work on the data from regression to neural networks and deep learning. 

Another reason the value of data is not static revolves interesting economics around data which also multiply it's value. Bill Schmarzo author of various books within the area of data introduces something he calls the *economic multiple effect*. The effect he discusses takes departure in economic theory and what it teaches about the intersection of supply and demand in a market economy. The price or value of any good is in a market economy self-regulated by the forces of supply and demand. More demand or lower supply leads to upwards price pressure and vice versa. What if we consider data as a good or a product? In many ways there are is demand for data due to the effects discussed in the prior sections. 

The supply side comes from the systems that generate data. Increasing the volume of data generated and collected should according to economic theory decrease the value of data however this is where data goods are somewhat different. When we have data from different systems or in greater detail it's not just one product. Instead they are often complementary which means there is a synergy effect where the value of existing data goods produced is increased. What happens when we introduce more complementary data is that we capture more of the contextual view of the enterprise. In some sense when each new data source can be puzzled together with existing data to increase the quality of information it may scale to all the existing data and information products. Examples include complementing data from a IoT devices with master data and transactional data or HR systems with CRM systems and so on. 

Carl Anderson the author of *Creating a Data–driven Organization* also presents this perspective. Where he explains how the value of data can multiply by enriching the existing contextual view of the world as it is. That should be motivating and likely also one of the motivations that leaders have when investing in their data and IT infrastructure. With the advancements in technology and the growing IT-landscape in businesses have caused a synergistic effect where both terms of $A$ are rising at once, which is how the value of data doesn't just grow — it compounds. Businesses that have built their data foundation have greater context and with every new feature such as machine learning the value of using those tools are even stronger. This evolvement will continue and the reason why we see many companies invest in their data and IT infrastructure as they know this will become even more valuable down the line.
