While i have introduced some tools, mindsets and habits that can help achieve better success with data, there is no substitute for hard work and experience. Getting practice and acquiring data literacy is something that does not come over night and not by itself. It requires work and we've been through some practical examples of how to gain expertise that helps you built knowledge factories. Data management, design and implementation with data cycle expertise. Practicing these areas is cultivating creativity something which Wouter Groeneveld investigates in his book *The creative programmer*. He defines creativity as ones ability to problem solve. His investigation leads to the argument that technical knowledge is a key component for elevating one's creativity and quality of work. I can think of a few examples from several lines of work. For example 

1) The chef that tastes food knowing exactly what spice to add, 
2) The sports coach that change the formation or make other tactical dispositions in the middle of the game or 
3) The car mechanic that just by looking and listening to the sound of the car knowns exactly what bolts to tighten. 
To me these are all examples of creativity and they come with extensive technical knowledge and experience.  

Gaining technical knowledge in the manufacturing of knowledge and data-centric solutions can also be practiced. Someone like Matthew Benham seems to posses many such skills, perhaps most importantly he was not only talking about data, he actually put it to productive use in innovative ways no one had seen before. Benham's mathematical and statistics background is an example of technical knowledge which likely gave him that ounce of creativity needed. He found ways to *generate* data which didn't exists and to bring the data all the way from generation to the people who make critical day-to-day, season-to-season decisions. Thus he had to go through the data cycle integrate raw data, transforming and serving it. 

There is a host of fundamental skills which we can practice to give ourselves a better chance of imitating this success story. 

**Practicing the fundamentals**
We might divide the set of fundamental skills into *technical* and *non-technical*. 
What it means is that we should practice foundational technological skills such as computer science and particularly those that touch data systems. That includes databases, software engineering and so on. Building upon the computer science we have concepts such as data warehouse architecture and how to design elegant data journeys from ingestion, transformation and serving. 

That goes into understanding businesses and their systems. Talking to business people and domain experts emphasizing with them through diagramming business processes and mapping systems is a big part of that. We should practice data management to take responsibility of long term success with data by having good practices around data such as quality and metadata management. We should practice data modeling at all levels to understand how the systems produce and generate data that can be captured. Finally we need to go the last mile of ingesting, transforming and serving data in iterative loops such that it becomes part of systems and produce valuable results. 

By learning new technologies, systems thinking and decision theory you are able to push new ideas down the value chain. By being in contact with stakeholders in the organization you can pull ideas from bottom up. This is a nice balance that has to be found between formal requirements gathering and coming up with new ideas proactively. For example the invention of the radio was credited to ... but actually it was some rather abstract and experimental discoveries that led concepts like electromagnetism and hertz that allowed this design. That's why we should keep experimenting and learning in combination with asking and emphasizing with business people. 

As data professionals much of the work we do can be categorized as *knowledge work*. It means that our knowledge is what produces our work and how we provide value for businesses. There are some fundamental technical knowledge which has persisted the times of change in this area of work. Some of these include how to model data, how to use tools to perform tasks related to the data lifecycle activities. In addition there are some "softer" technical skills such as critical thinking, systems thinking and decision theory. Being able to make smart decisions is important, particularly if you get managerial responsibilities. Honing the skills to identify misinformation, noise and fallible arguments are important for this.
