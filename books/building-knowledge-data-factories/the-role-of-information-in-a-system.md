In Donella Meadows book *thinking in systems* a Dutch experiment caught my attention. She refers to this experiment in the section of her book on information and systems. In this experiment households where fitted with electric meters but with the catch that in one group the meters were installed in the basement of the house and in the other group they were installed in the front hall. 

First of all we can look at a household as a system. It has several elements such as furniture, equipment and family members. Family members are using furniture and equipment such as kitchen appliances or taking a hot shower. These elements could be powered and heated by electricity at least that is how it works in most parts of the world. Electric meters installed in the house introduces *information* about the energy use in to the system, like data about a player does to a scout. In the first group the Information entered the system in a natural way neither distorted, delayed or withheld in any way. In the other group the experiment was designed to create a lot of friction for the information to enter the system. 

In a system information that enters can create a *feedback loop*. Feedback loops are *"closed chains of casual connections from a stock, through a set of decisions or rules or physical laws or actions that are dependent on the level of stock, and back again through a flow to change the stock"* (Donella Meadows, page 25). In figure 1.1 the feedback loop mechanism is visualized. It shows information is circulated in the feedback loop which allows the system to observe it and adjust it's behaviors and structure over time moving the system towards it's purpose.

We can now also better understand the problem with meters in the basement. The feedback loop was broken by poor design. That meant information never enters the system or it does so with a lot of friction which is unwanted. In the other group with meters in the front hall, the feedback loop could take effect. Most households naturally want to reduce energy consumption and every walk past the electric meter guided the household about the effectiveness of their actions and behaviors. This input allowed them to learn and adjust and this explains why this group reduced their energy consumption 30 percent more than the other at the end of the experiment. From this experiment get a sense of what is important when building data and information products. Particularly it's important how we design solutions that take into account not only the quality of the information (fit for purpose) but also the entire system so that we ensure information *enters* the system with as little friction as possible. Similarly there is also a question frequency and value of *real-time* data. If the electric meters only were updated monthly, the feedback loop would be weaker. This experiment highlight the important role of information in systems and one of the reasons Meadows writes that.

The definition of a feedback loop introduced some new concepts related to systems thinking, namely *stocks* and *flows*. These are important concepts that help us understand even more the role of information in systems. 
### Monitoring stocks and adjusting flows
Donella Meadows writes how "*a stock is the foundation for any system. Stocks are the elements of the system that you can see, feel, count or measure at any given time*". That definition naturally grabbed my attention because counting, measuring and monitoring is synonymous with the words metrics and KPIs. I've encountered these words frequently as a data professional. We have already encountered a stock in the Dutch experiment, where it was the stock of energy consumption. In a system there are stocks we want to maximize, minimize or just regulate. For example a household want to minimize the stock of energy consumption subject to constraints of having to complete regular household chores and enjoying a hot shower.

Another concept of systems are flows. Perhaps even more interesting flows are what causes stocks to change over time. As Meadows write *"It is the fillings, purchases and sales, deposits and withdrawals, successes and failures. A stock then, is the present memory of the history of changing flows within the system"* (p. 18). Now this is really interesting. What causes the stock of energy consumption are the flows. To name a few inflows it could be turning on the light, opening the window on a cold day with the radiator turned on, turning on the oven, turning on hot water, leaving the fridge open and so on. Outflows on the other hand is all the opposite actions. Flows are really interesting to study because these are the behaviors and leverage points which we when understood greatly can help get stocks to desired levels. 

In this way information helps the system adapt and organize itself in better ways. 


For example if we measure sales and profit as a stock then the flows are the behaviors and mechanisms that influence the level of the stock. Perhaps a new marketing campaign causes the stock of sales to rise, but at the same time the costs creates an outflow of cash and profits. If the sum of those two forces is net positive, it creates more profit. 

The reason managers want to track sales, expenses and profits can now be explained quite reasonably. The stock of sales and profits is a factual measure linked to the purpose of the firm. Being directly tied to the purpose it provides the managers clear feedback about the effectiveness of the collective behavior of the firm. 

However identifying the levers of which flow can be generated to increase these stocks can be challenging and is perhaps the most important management task. Many organizations are aware that overall sales and profit performance is a lagging indicator. It might be used to trace back how well teams have been performing on fundamental business disciplines such as operations, marketing, human relations, management, finance and so on. Financial data can therefore be a good starting point, but requires deeper analysis to tie it to the success of behaviors of the day-to-day business. That is why many businesses built data platforms which can integrate data from multiple data sources to provide a more complete contextual view of the enterprise. For example departments might have their own specific set of data points which they care about. 

The marketing department may naturally be interested in conversion rates, customer satisfaction, retention and churn rates and the overall stock of sales. These are stocks related to the effectiveness of their behavior and activities which provides more concrete feedback they can use to adjust. In the HR-department they are interested in stocks like employee satisfaction while the operations department want to monitor the quality of products and so on. In that way the systems and subsystems of the business have their own important stocks to monitor where they use their specialized knowledge to adjust and learn. For a household that wants to reduce it's energy consumption it might be quite obvious that actions like closing windows on a cold day, turning down the heat on a warm day and reducing less hot water are productive. For a business that chain-of-logical thought and reasoning might be less obvious and requires more experimentation and subject matter expertise. 

So there we have the *crux* of managing and running businesses with data. 

> Capturing, collecting and measuring important stocks in the business systems so agents are informed and through that feedback loop can use that updated knowledge to learn and adjust behaviors that through inflows and outflows move stocks towards desired levels.

In other words "*systems thinkers view the collections of system stocks and see the mechanisms for regulating them via the flows*" (p.  25).
