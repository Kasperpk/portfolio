It has been a key challenge to master the data science discipline for businesses since the advancement of computer and information technology began rolling. This challenge has inspired many professionals and been a topic of discussion for many authors.

One of the industry gems on the topic is the *data warehouse toolkit methodology* by Ralph Kimball which was first published in the 1990s. In this book Kimball suggests a roadmap for building a data warehouse and have since released a couple of versions which rely on decades of experience of building data warehouses. Another prominent figure in the industry is Bill Inmon. Inmon wrote books in the early era of the industry among them the book *Building a data warehouse* first published in 1992. Both authors contributed with a methodology to *operationalize* managing and running businesses with data. 

A more recent contribution by Bill Schmarzo introduces what he calls the *analytical maturity index* (figure 5.1) Schmarzo uses this index to discuss the kinds of obstacles and challenges that meets business that want to succeed and level up their analytical maturity. Most organizations are somewhere on that journey working towards a goal that constantly moves and so the goal is really just about getting started. The first obstacle that Schmarzo identifies is access to essential hardware and software. This is of course relevant because without any database or application software to generate data, the journey cannot even begin. Kimballs methodology also emphasize making an assessment of the business as one of the first steps. Here he writes that it among other things also involves considering *"Technical or resource feasibility, but data feasibility is the most crucial. Are we collecting real data in real operational source systems to support the business requirements? Data feasibility is a major concern because there is no short-term fix if we’re not already collecting reason ably clean source data at the right granularity."* (p. 335)

Another obstacle is to find the ways to activate data value which we can be called *requirements gathering*. Kimball emphasize that understanding the business is one of the key elements in building a successful data warehouse in the first place. Pulling requirements from the desired out comes and business problems is like working *backwards*. Many engineers might forget this and instead try to work *forwards* with the technology available building all kinds of things that just does not solve a problem. Instead the requirements gathering is about starting from a desired end and tracking back a solution which may or may not be based wholly or partly on a data product in a data warehouse.

I like the working backwards approach because we may find a solution to a problem even though it will not be based on a data product. With that said i think the occasional working forwards can sometimes actually inspire and find new requirements. This is perhaps why Inmon relaxed the assumptions a bit. Kimball relies on quite formal and comprehensive interview and workshop activities to really understand the business problems and how people actually do the work. Inmon  questioned the ability of users to define their needs beforehand. In that way he advocated also for working *forwards* without having defined all requirements upfront and i think the occasional technology push forward can be useful.

Even with great hardware, software and well defined business problems there might be obstacles in the way of greater analytical maturity. Schmarzo put *culture* as the third obstacle which led me to Carl Anderson's book *Creating a Data-Driven Organization: Practical Advice from the Trenches*. Anderson does equally mention prerequisites like technologies to collect, store and transform data. Likewise does he mention the importance of collection data *fit for purpose* with a link between the data we collect and what we need in the business. In addition he spends a whole chapter on culture. He defines *data-drivenness* as *"building tools, abilities, and, most crucially, a culture that acts on data"*.

Culture is connected to mindset, skills and capabilities of workers and it's something i will return to here [[3 The right people and culture]]. Anderson emphasize the importance of having people that "*asks the right questions of the data, who have the skills to extract the right data and metrics and use that to inform next steps*". 

A lot of work has been done in this area and many have contributed with their own frameworks or entire *data-driven operating model*s (DDOMs). A DDOM is a model that describes the elements needed in order to design data-driven businesses. Kimball and Inmon's pioneering methodologies can be considered some of the first DDOMs. Figure 1.2 shows one version where three pillars insights requirements, data management and team enablement needs to be managed.

Like Kimball and Inmon's methodologies this model includes requirements gathering as a core piece. Their methodologies include more than requirements. They also emphasize how to piece data together and how to implement solutions often following the phases of ingestion, transformation and serving. We can consider this being part of the data management bubble in figure 1.2. Finally this model includes team enablement. What this leg suggest is that there is an element of having the right skills and competencies in place in order to succeed. Like any discipline in business to succeed it takes talent and specialized resources to succeed. 

The DDOM in figure 1.2 is not the only way to view the operationalization problem. *Boston Consulting Group* similarly offer a model that they use to guide their clients that want to start a data transformation. Going top-down this model begins with vision and overall strategic thoughts about the role of data and information in the organization. They begin with a vision what we might call a data strategy which is often found in connection with data management and governance. In the next box we find use cases and that is similar to requirements insights from figure 1.2. The analytics layer investigate how well the team is equipped with the necessary competencies and how to ensure the data is going to be embedded and integrated into the systems, culture and processes. Governance and infrastructure are also data management tasks and so the Boston Consulting Group has divided the data management across different boxes.

What both DDOMs emphasize is that operationalizing the data discipline in organizations involves some element of picking the right stocks to monitor. Then findings ways to enable it with teams and skills to put it into the organizational systems and finally deliberate efforts to manage the infrastructure and processes around it. 

Managing the whole apparatus of succeeding with data, putting consistent practices into place, enabling teams and developing the right IT and data infrastructures is what can be put into the data management pillar. 

In the rest of this chapter we will try to hone in one several aspects of what has been identified as the core pillars of managing and running businesses on data. Particularly we have found that there are different elements which is important, such as. 

* Having the right technologies through a well functioning IT and data infrastructure
* Understanding the business and built solutions from well defined problems
* Creating the right culture and the right people that have the skills to unlock technology and ask the right questions
* Having a management discipline that oversees all of these activities focusing on the big picture and long-term sustainability

We will particularly focus on understanding the business and building solutions from well defined problems. Here we will try to use some of the learnings from the first chapter.
