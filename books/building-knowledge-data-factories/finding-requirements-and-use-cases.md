In this chapter we will focus particularly on the challenge of working and using data, that is the ability to activate data value. As discussed in the previous part this comes down to identifying business problems and working backwards from there until we might find a potential solution that involves data and information in some shape or form.

We know that data has the ability to generally increase the utility of decisions both one-off and repeating, but also to make long-term learning and adjustment take place in systems through feedback loops. We somehow to connect those dots. 

Looking for inspiration Matthew Benham pops into my mind. With a background in mathematics and statistics Benham have most likely understood the value of data and *raw numbers*. He probably also had a liking for football and so he knew the *problems* and was a domain expert. This is a strong combination which probably can explain a lot of the success he has had. Having this strong academic background and special interest for football he and his team identified key pieces of data that were most informative when it came to predicting matches in football. This data was not available anywhere. So what did Benham do? Well they set up a team of watchers to record this data. By understanding the business problem in-depth and connecting it to a solution with data and information as input, the solution came together. They had *watchers* at games capturing important events such as number of offensive passes, ball possession, shots on target, runs behind the defensive line and so on. This is why it's important to work backwards. Because none of this was possible with the current data and technology available, but motivated by a well defined business problem the pieces came together.

As we have studied the value proposition of data we have also been studying mathematics and statistics. Like Benham we can use this to appreciate the potential of *raw numbers*, good books and great advice as we can see the mathematical relationship between utility and access to information. We've also been studying systems thinking which helps understand the whole system in which the data and information partake. This helps us see the importance of designing a system so that *agents* receive the information. 

By combining these perspectives and i recommend continuing to study them we can like Benham put ourselves in a position to built valuable products with data. The value of SmartOdds is evidential because it was generating a lot of business and so in some way it must have activated data value. We can imagine it marginally reducing uncertainty and risk in decision important enough to be considered. 
## Working backwards
The main motivation should come from understanding a business problem very well. What i suggest here is an approach designed to help us work backwards solving a real business problem perhaps by activating the mechanisms with which data creates value.

Many engineers typically find themselves part of a larger project management structure such as Scrum or Kanban. There is a product owner who brings, orders, and prioritizes the backlog and so on. When in this setup it it's natural to "slip" into a rather passive role and there can be good reasons for this. Particularly when there are subject matter experts who are able to define productive work for us. For example such as Benham and his team that identified important data points to collect in football games. The downsides of the passive roles is that we solely rely on others to bring ideas and work to us. 

Secondly we might fail to think critically about how the request is actually activating value of data. It's not the project management structure that are the problem a well-run backlog is still a reasonable place to work from. What this approach changes is the posture of the engineer working inside that structure. Equipped with systems thinking and decision theory, we are no longer only executing whatever sits at the top of the backlog. We can ask why an item belongs there, push back when a request is not actually justified by any mechanism that activates the value proposition of data, and, as our domain literacy deepens, begin bringing items to the backlog ourselves rather than only taking from it. Even if we're so lucky as to have subject matter experts filling up our task board we don't have to fall into passiveness. 

We should ask questions that allow us to understand the business problem we are solving. By this deep understanding we are equipped with a better understanding of how to design the solution so it has the capability of activating feedback loops and greater utility for *agents* in the systems. A second proactive step is to actively work on our literacy in the various domains we are supplying solutions for. For example if you're working with the marketing or finance department, read and learn about it. This helps understand the business and become domain experts ourselves like Matthew Benham's football knowledge. Then we can work backwards and connect these problems to what we know about information in systems and decision making.

Becoming a subject matter expert ourselves is perhaps one of the most productive things we can do for our ability to produce valuable data product designs. So the first part suggest learning about the business you are working for. 

We can read about it but as every business has their own uniqueness it can be very helpful to talk to people within the organization about their work or to actually get yourself in the shoes of those who performs the work. Those conversations help understand what is important to accomplish and why. It helps identifying elements, interconnections and purpose so we can apply our systems thinking and decision theory concepts.  

The conversational aspect is important, particularly in early stages when you try to learn and need to upskill yourself to a subject matter expert. This is where our questions matter. Good questions are open-ended and focused on the things we are trying to understand. So it could be something like. 

* Describe what you're doing in the first hour of your day?
* What kind of information is sent to you everyday?
* What would you like to measure?
* How are things moved around in the department? both physically and digitally. 
* The best days at work are when?
* What are the biggest challenges at work you had lately?

Sit with the people for a while and try to map out what happens. It can be process diagrams, systems map of interconnections, elements and purpose. After some talks you might then step back and perhaps read some more or observe it from another angle. Things should be looked through the lens of systems thinking and decision theory because the purpose here is still to enable the value proposition of data. 

This process might go on for a while where you go back, talk some more, read some more and update your diagrams and understanding. Going deeper into the structure of the systems with stocks, flows and leverage points. Becoming subject matter expert requires time and patience. It's also likely that one can only have one or two such areas of deep expertise which is why larger organizations should employ enough people so one individual can be dedicated to one or two areas at max. 

Once we have our process going we might start to identify some problems. We can then built a backlog which throughout describe this problem. Then from there we have can try to describe a desired outcome or solution. This process requires us to trace back, look for problems to similar solutions and to ask questions about until we understand it deeply. This is where the bulk of our time and work is actually spent.

With the problem understood throughout we can begin to develop a plan for how to solve it. It can be a business process which is running inefficiently with a lot of manual paper work. It could be a sales person who needs information about the effectiveness of the sales efforts or which products are in high demand. These are problems which has connection to data and information products, however there can be many different kinds of problems. 

The problem is clearly defined and understood and now we have a potential solution or at least some idea which includes a data and information product. 
## Designing data and information products
Designing the data and information product is really the next part of the exercise. Here we draw on experience, on seeing related products and from what we can imagine. 

 When product design teams shipping or launching a new physical product or digital service they are in a similar situation. They first of all need to find new products that it resonates with a customer group. A product by itself does not become a success unless it is serving a need in sufficiently high demand by solving a real problem. In the same way, data products don't succeed unless it activates data value through the means we studied in the first part. Without it, it could seriously hurt profitability and the brand equity of the company. 

Organizations do this through market research which aims to develop a deep understanding of customers. They might use the concept of *personas*. A persona describes the potential *user* of a product. It could be there circumstances and their day-to-day whereabouts and more specifically. It's here that the product design team then use this to design products which fulfill some need. A lot of this research focus on *empathizing* with the users as we've seen many unsuccessful products launched because of assuming.  

When we design the solution we can learn is the *product* design mindset. We can swap out *users* with *agents* operating in the business *systems*. We can use personas to describe their work, the business problem and how then our data product fits in. We can describe how our product fits in to the system, how it creates feedback loops or otherwise activate value by introducing information into the system and decisions made by agents within it. The person presenting is an example of a persona or agent which has to use some specific data, at a specific time an presented in a specific way.

A description of a certain persona or agent within the system might be *the product owner*. 
"The product owner is responsible for a single department and all the tasks which are put to the central IT and data teams. It's important for him to monitor the stocks of *hours worked* on the tasks on a weekly basis as the lead metric for progress on the important *projects* currently work in progress. Every week the product owner has to present these numbers to the management team so they can adjust any potential *inflows* or *outflows* that might effect the important stock of *hours worked on important projects*. They evaluate project progress both quantitative and qualitatively as they discuss anything that might have blocked or advanced progress".

With this description and a sketch of how the agent here the product owner we have a use-case founded in deeper understanding the system and what the data actually helps accomplish. Here number of hours worked on projects is a stock which provides a *feedback loop* that helps the management team to *decide* on what initiatives to take, adjusting the *flows* that might impact this stock. 

We now have both a clearly and well understood business problem and a case for how to solve it. This means we can create the plan for implementing the solution. Sometimes we might create a smaller prototype depending on how sure we are about the actual problem and solution. A prototype can then reveal things we might not have understood. That feedback provides ideas to adjust, add or remove something. Along the way i suggest keeping a bit of documentation for the solution. The documentation should describe the overall idea, the systems it exists to support and how *agents* use it and to what purpose. The document can be fit it into a logical domain such as sales, HR, operations, finance, marketing and so on. 

An important part of being able to proactively contribute is that we keep learning about systems thinking, decision theory and technical tools. This enables us to both question the ideas and projects we become part of but also push ideas to the table., transforming the role from a passive to an active one. 

At the same time when we read, learn and observe on subjects related to the business our insights are strengthened. Collaborating with subject matter experts can be a helpful exercise where questions and conversations can fast-track our learning. This parallel track of learning enable us to ask the right questions, understand system, bring ideas and to perhaps create innovative solutions like Matthew Benham.

The ability to identity problems and come up with great solutions is probably the number one skill. However as we have seen there are typically other elements that make an impact on long-term success with data. In the next section we will look into data management which is a discipline that understands that this is a ongoing problem and project for organizations. It's a discipline that need continuous support, resources and deliberate efforts.
