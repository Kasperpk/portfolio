# The Edsel of data

Here is a Ford Edsel. What a wonderful sight. How could anyone not want to drive to school in this one? In the mid-1950s, Ford's management believed they had the answer to General Motors and Chrysler: a bold new brand that would carve out its own space in the market. They poured vision, confidence, and staggering sums of money into the project.

![The Ford Edsel Pacer, 1958. order_242 from Chile, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/57/Edsel_Pacer_1958_%284922383186%29.jpg)

*Figure 1.1 The Ford Edsel. Source: order_242. Edsel Pacer 1958. Photograph. 2010. [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Edsel_Pacer_1958_(4922383186).jpg). CC BY-SA 2.0.*

On "E-Day," September 4, 1957, the Edsel was unveiled with great fanfare. Ford executives promised it was the result of sophisticated market analysis, research, and a deep understanding of what consumers wanted. Investors were assured this car would redefine the family automobile. To prove their commitment, Ford spent roughly $400 million on product development, advertising, and the nationwide launch. In the late 1950s, that was no small sum — it was an investment that practically demanded success.

But the Edsel didn't succeed. Quite the opposite. Instead of being remembered as Ford's triumph, it became a symbol of commercial failure. Sales sputtered, production was discontinued in 1960, and the very name "Edsel" became shorthand for a flop. Marketers began using it in case studies of how not to launch a product.

Why did it fail? The later postmortem revealed something surprising: Ford's "extensive" market research wasn't so extensive after all. The company never really tested how buyers would respond to the Edsel's radical styling or whether families wanted what Ford thought they wanted. What was billed as data-driven decision-making turned out to be little more than guesswork wrapped in confidence. The result: an estimated $250 million loss and a very sour taste in the mouths of investors, especially since Ford had just gone public in 1956.

Not every failure was as catastrophic as the Edsel. Take Heinz's attempt to reinvent ketchup at the turn of the millennium. In 2000, they launched the brightly colored "EZ Squirt" line — green, purple, even blue ketchup — and, amazingly, it worked. Kids loved the novelty, sales spiked, and for a while it seemed like Heinz had tapped into a new market.

![Three Heinz EZ Squirt bottles in green, red, and purple — the coloured ketchup line launched in 2000 and discontinued in 2006.](<Heinz EZ .png>)

*Figure 1.2 The Heinz EZ Squirt.*

But the success was short-lived. By 2006, the product was discontinued. Parents increasingly questioned whether anyone really wanted artificial coloring in their food, and the novelty wore off. It wasn't a financial disaster on the scale of the Edsel, but it exposed the same blind spot: a failure to understand what customers truly valued in the long run.

And of course, the list of commercial flops goes on. Amazon's Fire smartphone, Microsoft's Zune, even Cheetos-flavored lip balm all serve as reminders that having resources, distribution, and brand recognition is no guarantee of success.

## The Edsel's of data

While I've never been involved in designing physical products, all of the above examples explain the dangers of not having a rigorous process for design, prototyping, and user testing. Launching a large-scale product without a clear market can have catastrophic consequences for financial health and brand equity. Luckily, Ford, Heinz, Microsoft, and Amazon survived their flops — and their stories now serve as cautionary tales for the rest of us.

The same risks exist in data and analytics. In fact, I'd argue there are more similarities between physical product design and data product design than there are differences. In a typical data cycle, we're sourcing something raw, enriching it by integrating, adding, and molding it. Visit any manufacturing company and you'll see a similar process: materials flow through intermediate stages before becoming finished goods. In the data world we call this ELT or ETL, sometimes structured into layered models like the medallion architecture.

The Edsel's of data are the solutions that, despite effort and resources, don't actually make the user's life better. Maybe the design team didn't go the last mile to ensure usability. Maybe the product was oversold as the "next big thing." Or maybe the data itself was never truly molded to fit the purpose. The financial hit might not reach the same scale as Ford's $250 million Edsel failure, but wasted cycles in data projects still cost dearly — in time, money, and trust.

So maybe the lesson of the Edsel isn't just about chrome, fins, or misplaced confidence. It's about humility in design — whether we're talking about cars, ketchup, or data. Skip the hard work of testing assumptions, listening to users, and iterating before scaling, and you risk building a beautiful failure. Treat every prototype as a conversation with the people who will use it, and suddenly the odds shift. Do that well, and we not only avoid creating the Edsel of data — we might just build something that feels timeless.
